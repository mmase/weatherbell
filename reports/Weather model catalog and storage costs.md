# Bundled tiles make a full catalog cheap

Isobar can ingest and render a combined WeatherBell + Weathermodels.com catalog of roughly 45 models and datasets for about **$350–400 per month in pipeline cost (storage writes, compute, hot storage)**. That holds only if map tiles are written as one bundled object per field per forecast hour. Written as one object per tile, the same workload costs about **$15,000–16,000 per month**, almost all of it in PUT fees. At that point delivery, not ingest, becomes the dominant cost. Serving about 30 TB/month of tiles costs about $3,500/month through CloudFront pay-as-you-go and close to $0 in egress from Cloudflare R2. The upstream side is large but free to read. Measured on 2026-09-27, the AWS open-data mirrors of the catalog's models carry **about 15 TB/day** of raw files, led by ECMWF open data (3.2 TB/day), GEFS (2.7 TB), HAFS (2.3 TB in season) and GFS (1.8 TB). Reading only the needed GRIB messages through `.idx` byte ranges cuts that by roughly 500x per field. Two items could move the budget more than any cloud pricing choice: WeatherBell's 0.1° hourly ECMWF product, which the open 0.25° feed does not provide, and whether all members of the ENS files are ingested. The competitor catalogs themselves are only partly verified. Every WeatherBell and Weathermodels.com page was blocked by the research environment's egress proxy, so model and parameter lists come from search-engine summaries and indexed URLs, not the live menus.

Weathermodels.com still describes itself as an ECMWF "maximum charge customer" ([ECMWF Guidelines](https://weathermodels.com/index.php?r=site/ecmwf-guidelines)). That wording belongs to the old tariff and is probably outdated. The practical catch is resolution. The free AWS feed is 0.25° at 3-/6-hourly steps. Matching WeatherBell's 0.1° hourly-to-90 h product needs the 9 km stream, which is not on AWS today. The notes estimate it at about 6.4x the 0.25° point count, which is about 0.9 GB per HRES step for the same parameters. ECMWF service charges for that feed are unknown.

## Pipeline cost is about $0.009 per thousand field-hours when tiles are bundled

The base unit is one 2D field for one forecast hour of GFS 0.25° (1440×721 points). It covers zooms z0–z3, which is **85 tiles or about 2.5 MB at 30 KB per tile**. One field-run is 209 forecast hours, 17,765 tiles and about 0.53 GB. At list prices, the costs for one such field-hour are as follows. A single bundled R2 PUT costs $0.0000045 at $4.50 per million Class A writes ([Filebase](https://filebase.com/blog/cloudflare-r2-pricing-costs-savings-and-alternatives-in-2026/)). Compute of about 0.5 CPU-seconds costs $0.0000018 on c7g spot at $0.2108/h for 16 vCPU ([DoiT](https://www.doit.com/compute/compute/aws/us-east-1/c7g.4xlarge)). Two days of hot R2 storage cost $0.0000025. That totals **about $0.0000088 per field-hour, or $0.0018 per GFS field-run**. Writing each tile as its own object replaces one PUT with 85, which raises the cost to **$0.00039 per field-hour and about $0.08 per field-run, a 44x increase**. The 0.5 CPU-s and 30 KB figures are unbenchmarked assumptions. No citable decode or tiling benchmarks were found for eccodes, wgrib2 or the Rust decoders ([gribberish](https://github.com/mpiannucci/gribberish/blob/main/python/README.md); [gribtract](https://github.com/jedarden/gribtract)). Ingest reads are free because the NOAA and ECMWF buckets are sponsored open data. That sponsorship status is also unverified for 2026.

To scale this unit to the catalog, each model is converted into "GFS-equivalent field-hours per day": forecast steps per day, times tile count relative to GFS (tile count grows with grid points and max zoom), times assumed map layers. The step counts come from the measured cadences above. The tile factors and layer counts are Isobar planning assumptions, not sourced facts. Ensembles are costed as about 20–40 derived layers (mean, spread, probabilities), not as individually rendered members.

At that scale the pipeline writes about **3.4 billion tiles a month**. It produces about **3.4 TB/day of tile output**, and keeping two days hot requires a store of about 6.7 TB. Bundled, the $357 splits into about $182 of R2 PUTs (40 million objects), about $101 of hot storage, and about $74 of spot compute (5,600 vCPU-hours). The same compute costs about $180 on Fargate Graviton at $0.032/vCPU-h ([LeanOps](https://leanopstech.com/blog/aws-ecs-fargate-pricing-2026/)), or one or two Hetzner dedicated servers, whose 2026 prices rose 3–21% ([Hetzner](https://www.hetzner.com/pressroom/standardization-and-price-adjustment-of-our-server-products/)). On S3, storage rises to about $155 and PUTs to about $200 at $0.005 per 1,000 (the PUT price is unverified). Per model-run-parameter, the bundled figures are about $0.0018 for a GFS field-run, about $0.005 for a 00z 0.1° ECMWF field-run (145 steps at 4x tiles), about $0.0009 for a 48 h HRRR field-run, and about $0.0001 for one MRMS radar frame at a single level. Four models alone make up more than half of all tile writes: ECMWF 0.1°, RRFS, NBM and HRRR. The table also reveals that raw upstream volume is a poor proxy for cost. GEFS and ECMWF ENS are the heaviest downloads but, rendered as derived products, are among the cheapest layers. NBM and MRMS are small on disk but heavy in tiles because of their hourly or 2-minute cadence and fine grids.

## Five architecture choices set the bill, not the model count

**Bundled tile writes are the dominant decision.** They change pipeline cost by a factor of about 44, from roughly $15.7k to $0.36k a month. One object per field per forecast hour, or a PMTiles-style archive read with range requests, removes the PUT-fee problem. It also keeps objects above the 128 KB threshold below which S3 Intelligent-Tiering never moves data to a cheaper tier ([CloudCostRoom](https://cloudcostroom.com/blog/s3-intelligent-tiering-when-it-saves-and-when-it-costs-more)). The same logic applies to point and meteogram data. It belongs in chunked Zarr-style time-series stores, not millions of tiny objects.

**Byte-range reads from `.idx` sidecars are the second.** A GFS 0.25° forecast-hour file averages 542 MB and holds about 600 GRIB messages ([S3](https://noaa-gfs-bdp-pds.s3.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2&prefix=gfs.20260927/00/atmos/)). Reading one field by HTTP range, as Herbie does ([Herbie docs](https://herbie.readthedocs.io/en/2025.10.0/gallery/noaa_models/gfs.html)), pulls about 0.3–1.5 MB instead. Byte-range reads do not cover everything. ECMWF packs all 51 ENS members into one 6.6 GB file per step, so ensemble products need either per-member range reads or a full download. The AWS mirror pushes the whole 00z ENS in about 12 minutes, roughly 0.8 GB/s, so download bandwidth must be sized for bursts, not daily averages.

**R2 versus S3/CloudFront decides delivery, the largest recurring cost.** R2 charges $0.015/GB-month and no egress in any storage class ([Filebase](https://filebase.com/blog/cloudflare-r2-pricing-costs-savings-and-alternatives-in-2026/)). Cloudflare's terms explicitly allow large non-HTML content served from R2 through its CDN ([Cloudflare terms](https://www.cloudflare.com/service-specific-terms-application-services/)). S3 internet egress runs from $0.09/GB down to $0.05/GB by volume ([EgressCost](https://egresscost.com/aws/)), and CloudFront pay-as-you-go is about $0.085/GB ([Perfsys](https://perfsys.com/blog/cloudfront-pricing-guide/)). An illustrative 5,000 subscribers pulling 200 MB/day is about 30 TB and 1 billion requests a month. That costs about $2.5k in CloudFront transfer plus about $1k in request fees. On R2, egress costs nothing and Class B reads cost at most about $360 even if every request misses the cache. CloudFront's new flat-rate plans do not solve this, because their request caps (10M on Pro, 125M on Business, 500M on Premium) bind long before the 50 TB transfer cap ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudFront/latest/DeveloperGuide/flat-rate-pricing-plan.html)). Backblaze B2 behind Cloudflare is a credible alternative, since egress to Cloudflare is free ([Shade](https://shade.inc/blog/backblaze-review-video-production)). Wasabi is not: its 90-day minimum storage duration bills two-day tiles as if they were kept 45 times longer ([Wasabi docs](https://docs.wasabi.com/docs/how-does-wasabis-minimum-storage-duration-policy-work)).

**Where the compute runs matters because ECMWF sits in eu-central-1.** The NOAA buckets are in us-east-1, while the ECMWF mirror is in Frankfurt (eu-central-1) ([AWS Open Data Registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/ecmwf-forecasts/)). Reading from sponsored open-data buckets should cost Isobar nothing in either region. Moving Isobar's own intermediate data between regions costs $0.02/GB ([EgressCost](https://egresscost.com/aws/cross-region-data-transfer/)). The clean design runs workers next to each source and writes both regions' output straight into one global R2 tile store, so nothing re-crosses the Atlantic. Latency argues the same way. ECMWF open data lands on AWS about 7.5 h after cycle time, compared with about 51 minutes for HRRR, so ECMWF processing is a separate, bursty workload.

**Deciding which heavy raw products to skip is the last lever.** A pipeline that skips HRRR `wrfnat` (429 GB/day), GFS native netCDF history (about 370 GB/day), GEFS `init` (about 117 GB per cycle), RTOFS 3-D `archv` tarballs (184 GB/day) and GFS-Wave station-spectra tarballs, and range-reads ENS members only for the fields it plots, brings a raw 15 TB/day down to the 2–4 TB/day "forecast-useful" subset. Per-field `.idx` reads then shrink it further. Raw GRIB should be deleted immediately after processing because the source buckets keep long rolling archives. Only irreproducible outputs such as analysis-hour tiles belong in cold storage, for example R2 Infrequent Access at $0.01/GB or S3 Glacier Instant Retrieval at $0.004/GB ([CloudZero](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/s3-pricing/)).

## What remains unverified before committing a budget

The competitor catalogs are the weakest input. No WeatherBell or Weathermodels.com page could be opened, so every model and parameter claim rests on search summaries and indexed URLs, some of which mixed in text from Pivotal Weather or Tropical Tidbits. From the reference list, none of the following could be confirmed on either site: OISST, CPC, HRAP, PRISM, CDAS, NOHRSC, SPC/WPC, HiResW ARW/FV3, RTOFS, WW3, AIGEFS, GEFS-extended maps, CMC extended, CMAQ, GEOS dust, HAFS, Hovmöller and teleconnection pages. AIGFS on WeatherBell rests on one mixed-source summary. Weathermodels.com showed no evidence of AIFS, JMA, RRFS, NBM, SEAS5 or soundings. Its "14-km EPS" label conflicts with ECMWF ENS having run at about 9 km since 2023. No full per-model parameter list exists for either site. Getting one requires a logged-in scrape of the maps.weatherbell.com menus and of weathermodels.com's `preview-linklist` pages. On the data side, the following were not measured: sizes and latency for DWD ICON, MSC, JMA, all NOMADS-only products, and the licensed ECMWF 9 km feed. The RRFS grid, REFS member count and distribution paths were also not measured (SCN 26-48 was unreadable), and neither was the AIGEFS bucket path. The RRFS go-live date itself is contested: 14 October in the latest SCN, 6 October per GribStream ([GribStream](https://gribstream.com/blog/noaa-rrfs-refs-operational-august-2026)). On the cost side, every unit price came from third-party 2026 summaries because official pricing pages were blocked. The S3 PUT/GET prices, the CloudFront request fee and the Open Data Sponsorship terms are all unverified. The layer counts, tile factors, 30 KB tile size, 0.5 CPU-s per field-hour and 200 MB/subscriber/day delivery load are working assumptions that should be replaced with Isobar's own benchmarks.

## Conclusion

The expected cost driver, raw weather data volume, turns out to be nearly irrelevant to Isobar's bill. The feeds are free to read, and the heaviest ones (ENS, GEFS, HAFS) shrink to a few derived layers. What costs money is the number of objects written and the bytes served to users. Supporting "all of" WeatherBell's and Weathermodels.com's catalogs is therefore an ingest-engineering and licensing problem more than a storage-budget problem. Bundled writes keep even a 45-model catalog in the hundreds of dollars a month. R2-class delivery keeps a growing subscriber base from turning into a per-gigabyte liability.

The real strategic exposure sits outside the cloud invoice. Parity with WeatherBell's flagship ECMWF offering needs the 0.1° hourly stream. That stream is the single largest tile producer in the catalog, it is not on AWS, and its delivery charge is unknown. Separately, the NCEP transition of 14 October 2026 removes NAM, SREF and HiResW from the catalog while adding RRFS at a larger tile footprint than HRRR. Isobar should confirm ECMWF's 9 km delivery terms and build RRFS ingest before chasing the long tail of unverified reference-list products.
